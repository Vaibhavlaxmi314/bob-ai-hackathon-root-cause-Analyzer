# Problem Statement

## Background

Modern supply chains depend on a vast, interconnected network of cargo vessels, commercial trucks, and specialised refrigerated containers — known as reefers — to move goods across the globe. To minimise warehousing costs, companies operate on tight, just-in-time schedules where every leg of a shipment must connect precisely to the next. This pressure is especially acute for temperature-sensitive goods such as vaccines, biologics, pharmaceutical ingredients, and fresh perishables like produce and dairy, which must remain within strict temperature ranges throughout their entire journey to remain safe and usable. A single break in the cold chain — even for a few hours — can render an entire shipment unsalvageable.

At the same time, global logistics networks are continuously exposed to unpredictable disruptions: sudden severe weather events, port strikes, geopolitical crises, and border closures. These disruptions do not affect one shipment in isolation — they cascade silently across hundreds of active shipments simultaneously, and the full impact is rarely visible until it is too late to act.

## The Problem

Supply chain operations are largely blind to the real-time impact of live disruptions. When a disruption occurs — a port closure, a major storm, or a border shutdown — logistics teams have no automated way to instantly identify which of their hundreds of active shipments are trapped or at risk. This disruption blindness forces operators into a slow, reactive scramble at exactly the moment they need to act fastest.

The problem compounds in four interconnected ways. First, there is no reliable link between a live disruption event and the specific shipment legs it affects, so teams cannot triage impact at scale. Second, even when an affected shipment is identified, finding an alternative route or carrier requires manually contacting carriers, checking availability, and comparing costs — a process that takes hours. Third, while high-priority shipments sit stranded, trucks, trailers, and containers in nearby regions sit completely idle, because there is no system connecting available fleet capacity to rerouting needs. Fourth, and most critically for temperature-sensitive cargo, cold chain excursions — where goods exceed their safe temperature window — are only discovered at the point of delivery, when it is already too late to intervene, save the cargo, or meet regulatory reporting obligations.

## Who is Affected

This problem directly impacts three groups at mid-to-large enterprise organisations:

- **Logistics Operations Managers and Fleet Dispatchers** at freight and third-party logistics companies who coordinate more than 500 active shipments across global routes at any given time. They bear the operational cost of disruptions and are responsible for keeping freight moving.

- **Cold Chain Compliance Officers** at pharmaceutical manufacturers, vaccine distributors, and food & beverage companies who are accountable for maintaining and proving temperature integrity across every shipment leg. A failed cold chain audit can result in regulatory penalties and product recalls.

- **Supply Chain Directors** at manufacturing companies and retail distributors who depend on on-time component and goods delivery to keep production lines and store shelves running. A delayed critical shipment can halt an entire factory floor.

## Why It Matters

**1. Skyrocketing Emergency Costs**
When a stranded shipment is discovered late, logistics teams must secure last-minute alternatives at spot-market rates that are 2x to 3x higher than standard contract rates. These reactive decisions also drain operator time and cause downstream delays that ripple through the entire supply network.

**2. Wasted Fleet Assets**
Idle trucks, trailers, and reefer containers generate zero revenue while accumulating standing costs. Equipment only creates value when it is moving cargo. The inability to match idle assets to disrupted routes wastes thousands of dollars in fleet capacity every day.

**3. Catastrophic Cold Chain Losses**
A single temperature excursion on any one leg of a shipment — whether from equipment failure, a delayed handover, or an unexpected reroute through an uncontrolled environment — can spoil cargo worth $500,000 or more. Because excursions are discovered only at delivery, companies cannot intervene, cannot salvage the goods, and face regulatory and contractual liability with no warning.

## Why Existing Solutions Fall Short

**1. Data is Fragmented Across Silos**
Current tracking tools operate in isolation. Live weather and geopolitical risk feeds exist in one external system. Truck and container locations live in a legacy Fleet Management System (FMS). Customer orders and shipment routes live in a separate Transportation Management System (TMS). IoT temperature logs from reefer units are stored in yet another platform. No existing tool connects these silos into a single operational picture.

**2. Response is Entirely Manual**
When a route is blocked, operators must manually look up alternate paths, call individual carriers to check capacity, and scan spreadsheets to find idle nearby equipment. This slow, human-driven process takes hours — or days — far too long to prevent damage when goods are already in transit.

**3. No Predictive or Proactive Intelligence**
Existing mapping and tracking systems only report current location. They do not look ahead to model how a bottleneck at one leg propagates into delays further down the route. They do not alert operators to temperature trends approaching threshold limits before a breach occurs. And they do not surface idle assets proactively — an operator must already know to look.
